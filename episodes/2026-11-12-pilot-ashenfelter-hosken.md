# Pilot script. Abstract-only; no figures. Style goes in [brackets]; tags like <short pause> and |backchannels| are Gemini TTS markup.

MAYA [curious, conversational]: Okay, so today's paper asks a question that sounds almost too simple. <short pause> After a merger goes through, what actually happens to prices?
THEO: And that's weirdly rare to check, right? We put enormous effort into predicting merger effects before the deal closes, |mm-hm|, and then almost nobody goes back to look.
MAYA: Right. This is Orley Ashenfelter and Daniel Hosken, Journal of Law and Economics, twenty ten. They study five consumer products mergers, and they've got retail scanner data, so they can watch actual prices move.
THEO [skeptical, leaning in]: Okay, but here's my first question. How did they pick the five? Because if you picked mergers at random, I'd guess most of them are harmless.
MAYA [emphatic]: That is the key design choice. They deliberately chose mergers that, from the public record, looked most likely to be problematic. <short pause> The enforcement margin. The deals an agency could plausibly have challenged.
THEO: So the sample is, on purpose, not representative.
MAYA: Exactly, and the authors say so. Because of that, what they're estimating is an upper bound on the likely price effect of completed mergers. Not an average across all mergers.
THEO: Hm. That's a much more careful claim than "mergers raise prices."
MAYA: <short pause> And the headline result: four of the five mergers were followed by some increases in consumer prices. The fifth had little effect.
THEO: Which cuts two ways, honestly. Even among the deals you'd worry about most, one of five did essentially nothing. |Right.| But four of five is not nothing.
MAYA [warm]: And that tension is exactly why the identification question matters. <short pause> Next time, we'll get into how you actually build the counterfactual, and what could go wrong with it.
